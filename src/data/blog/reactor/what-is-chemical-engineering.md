 **How to explain to someone what is, or what does a Chemical engineer do?**

In other professions such question is answered easily, but not in this case. And often get this question from friends and family or when attending literally any event not connected to science. And my answer varies quite a lot, even though I study chemical engineering for four years already, because I had still not made my mind on how to answer it properly.

## Definition
The definition that you can find on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineering) goes as follows: "Chemical engineering is an engineering field which deals with the study of the operation and design of chemical plants as well as methods of improving production. Chemical engineers develop economical commercial processes to convert raw materials into useful products. Chemical engineering uses principles of chemistry, physics, mathematics, biology, and economics to efficiently use, produce, design, transport and transform energy and materials. The work of chemical engineers can range from the utilization of nanotechnology and nanomaterials in the laboratory to large-scale industrial processes that convert chemicals, raw materials, living cells, microorganisms, and energy into useful forms and products. Chemical engineers are involved in many aspects of plant design and operation, including safety and hazard assessments, process design and analysis, modeling, control engineering, chemical reaction engineering, nuclear engineering, biological engineering, construction specification, and operating instructions."

I know, such a long description seems redundant, but I do feel it is necessary. If you had read the long description, you may have got a feeling that Chemical engineers do quite everything, and you are right. And if you think that we just specialize further into one type of process or maybe one type of technology, that is not true. I, as a future Chemical engineer, need to understand all the processes all the technology that is used today in big productions, but also legacy technology, because it is important to understand all and everything. To be truthful, this is why I chose this field. I wrote about it in previous blog post [here](https://alchemistsnotes.com/notes/reactor/journey-to-chemical-engineering/), where I wrote more about why I chose this path.

Chemical engineer needs to be well versed in many fields, as does description write. I need to have a deep understanding of chemistry, physics, engineering in general, biology (but I try to ignore this one:)), and most importantly math. If somebody tells you that you don't need to understand math in order to study engineering, and mainly Chemical engineering, they are lying to you. Math plays a huge part in everything you learn in this field. All my four years of studying this was spent making calculations and deriving all the equations used in thermomechanics, hydrodynamics, reactor engineering, all these topics are filled with math. It doesn't mean it is hard, because I think if you would give it few hours you would understand it.

## The core of Chemical engineering
It is easy to write a chemical reaction, all these fancy numbers and elements, all of this is pretty straight forward. But it is much harder to think of how to recreate some reaction in the lab, let alone in big production scale. Because at this point focus shifts from ...

Every reaction is different, every production is different.

This is actually where most innovations die. It all due to the scalability of the process. If you can't figure out how to scale up your production, your product is not viable and will likely not survive in an open sea of different product options. Design and usability is important, but just as important is how much and how quickly you can produce your innovation.
