I was recently inspired by this [blog post](https://mnemonics.neocities.org/#theelements) made by Mnemonics. In this post they talk about how great philosophers of Ancient Greece were among the first to propose the theoretical background of the alchemical theory of matter. This beautiful summary gave me an idea to dive deeper into the origins of ALCHEMY. 

But first, dear reader, you may have noticed that my posts so far were about everything. Why so? This fact is already stated in the [About me](https://alchemistsnotes.com/about-me/) section of this website, but I would like to reiterate my reasons. I am a chemistry girl, chemistry runs in my blood and I breathe chemistry. Chemistry is quite literally my whole life. I've set my life on an academic path, and hopefully it will lead to me becoming a professor or just becoming a full-time researcher. That is my dream. But back to the blog itself. I plan to write here about topics I feel are important to me, or that I feel the public knows little about. So, be prepared for more chemistry here :). And for future topics I plan to write about the history behind some of the most important chemical compounds and how people were making them before fancy machines and exact calculations. 

And so, to kick off with some chemical topics on this blog, we will start at the very beginning of chemistry and alchemy. Well, we could start even before that. Because chemistry is literally everything. If you ever hear somebody say: "Oh my god, this is so full of chemicals". Well, they would be right, but not for the reasons they think. Everything is made from atoms that create arrays or molecules, which then are expressed as all these different and exciting materials, products, compounds and much more. 

The first people to actually try to explain chemistry were alchemists. The word alchemy comes from the Arabic word (which is apparent because of the prefix al-) al-kimiya, where al- is "the" in Arabic and kimiya comes from the Ancient Greek word khemia meaning "cast together". This knowledge was brought to you by [this](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology_of_chemistry) Wikipedia page. This little etymology dive is very telling of what chemistry or alchemy really is. Because if you imagine a chemist being in their lab, with a white coat on, mixing something together to create a colorful reaction, you are about right. Chemistry is largely about putting different compounds together in order to create or transform them into something new. 

But how does philosophy tie into alchemy? Well, I would argue that all science comes from philosophy, because in order to create something new, you first need to observe the world around you to understand where to look for your answers. Also, how can you know what answer you are looking for if you don't know the question? It's the same as one of the plot points from the book *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* by Douglas Adams. Great hyperintelligent people asked the most powerful supercomputer for the answer to the ultimate question of "Life, the Universe, and Everything", but after seven and a half million years all they got was the answer 42. This answer means nothing if you don't know the question. 

And these early philosophers, mainly in Ancient Greece were thinking a lot about the world around them. Many "famous" philosophers made their contributions to alchemy. Among them Plato came up with the idea of four primordial substances, namely fire, earth, water, and air. These elements are further associated with the properties of hot, cold, moist, and dry in Aristotle's theory. You may remark that this has no real benefit to today's science, because we now know that everything around us is made from much more than these four elements. But just their curiosity and endless questioning led to huge discoveries, because they studied a lot about how matter transforms from one element to another. Alchemists conducted experiments, observed reactions, and developed techniques for working with substances, which are used to this day. Nowadays, alchemy has been reinterpreted by, for example, Carl Jung and his study of the human psyche. A lot of the symbolism created by alchemy can be reused to explain human experience, ego, and emotional processes. 

Alchemy is largely viewed as a pseudo-science by the public, but in reality, alchemy was a stepping stone for the bloom of chemistry and science as a whole. And I would argue that alchemy, same as chemistry, is underappreciated and misunderstood. I would further argue that this is due to the way chemistry is taught. I have a huge issue with the way chemistry is taught in high school. I was very fortunate to have a great teacher, whose enthusiasm infected me so much that I myself went to study chemistry rather than any other sciences. He made lectures understandable without needing to memorize useless stuff. He did a lot of experiments which I remember till this day, and because of that I understood the subject much better. But I often see, in the students I tutor, that I was among the luckiest ones. Because they are made to hate chemistry. All this useless information that doesn't connect with their current reality is why there is such a lack of chemistry students in colleges. They have to memorize all the properties of the materials even though it would make much more sense teaching them why these materials even possess such properties and how we found that out. Chemistry is not about memorization of the periodic table, it is a way of thinking, same as alchemy was. It is a philosophical doctrine of how to look at the world and understand it. 

You may say that teaching kids chemical theory might be too hard on them, but I disagree. From my tutoring experience kids are very susceptible to learning about Lewis structures in explaining chemical bonds, which in my opinion is the greatest tool in understanding material properties and why some compounds exist and others not. 

So, what is the take from this ramble? Rather than dismissing alchemy as just a search for the philosopher's stone, or a way to transform materials into gold, maybe we should learn from it. Many of their ideas might seem outlandish, but alchemy provided a rich soil from which chemistry was able to evolve. Many chemical reactions and laboratory tools were made then that I as a chemist can't imagine my life without. And rather than looking at the surface level, we should dig deeper into how they came to their conclusion, because as I said before, chemistry is not about elements but rather the philosophy and a way to interact and learn about the world around us.
