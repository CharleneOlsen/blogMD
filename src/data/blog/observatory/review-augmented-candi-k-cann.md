## My thoughts about the book
This book offers an insight on existing technology and their reframing in how they changed our lives.The book is separated into two parts: Life and Death. Life part brought up many interesting views on robots, AI and their effect on humanity as a whole. But many of the arguments for new technologies are rooted in viewing everything very optimistically rather than skeptically. For some, this might not be an issue but for me, it very much was. The Death part was focused on how human experience death in modern age. Author brings up games, which is a great tool to question ones view on death. Also, she brings up metaverse in a sense of how great and innovative this project is. Now we know that it was just a huge cash grab. But this is not an issue really, anybody can be mistaken when talking about new technology, but metaverse was doomed from the beginning, and it is strange to read passages on it knowing there were signs of shutting down for a while now. The end felt most coherent from the whole book. As an appendix there are legal documents or declarations about the body and your digital assets, which I think everybody should read and decide upon while they still can. 

Overall, the book has a potential of being a precursor for a deeper dive into technologies lifted there. But unfortunately, a lot of the book reads more as a surface level listing rather than an in-depth overview.

**page:** 29
Author gives examples on how Asians view robots as an extent to human care. She takes into consideration religion as one of the key reasons why Asians are more willing to accept technology particularly robots as part of every day life unlike us Europeans and Americans. 

One of the examples she uses is the Tibetan prayer wheel. The inanimate object serves as a religious tool, but also becomes an object of reverence because it can pray in ways humans cannot. The prayer is more "correct", because the wheel has on its surface correctly written mantras or sutras, and so when it is spinning it will emit into the world correct words. 

I use quotation marks because for me, this view is quite alien. Maybe, and most probably, my view is very much affected by the Christianity even though I am an [atheist](https://alchemistsnotes.com/notes/synthesizer/why-atheist/).

**page:** 30
She gives an argument, that robots would be better at religion because it can say the prayer without mistake thus be more efficient. I'm not sure, if that believe is a belief of the author, but I would argue that saying prayers isn't about saying it correctly. Prayer is about intention, about emotion and how it all moves you through the space. 

I would argue further that this is similar to writing an essay or writing right about anything. Of course, AI / robot is more efficient, it can write more, without misspellings or typos, so the text is better, right? Well, no, in my opinion. Because again, it's about intention. Yes, we make mistakes, but essay is not written just to be written. It is written with a purpose to convey the information that is dear to the writer. For example, what I do now. AI can write what I write right now, but I would say it won't just stop mid-reading to write its thoughts because it was provoked by what is written. 

The thing that separates me from AI is **intention**. AI won't create anything because it wants to, it will always create from the prompts made by someone. And furthermore, the creations of AI are not creatively unique. I will repeat this till my death, but AI can't be creative. When creating text, poem or a picture it pulls from the vast database and mushes the human creation into a blob that you then get in the chat. 

AI can't provoke, AI can't inspire a deeper meaning, because it has not lived. Humans create because of their past, present and future. We create from hope, from pain. I create my poems exclusively from my feelings. 

**page:** 31
She talks about how a roaming robot was approached by a child and their parent. Author says that the robot is more approachable because of "those large friendly eyes and friendly voice".

Isn't this a huge problem? We forget how to talk to other humans, one of the best abilities to have. I would much rather to approach a human being because their answer would be much more sophisticated than that of an AI. Also, talking to a staff of the airport will give you much clearer picture of the country you are visiting, their culture and their mannerism. 

Same as the cafés or restaurants. Of course sometimes you are in a rush or feeling particularly miserable so you use self-checkout or a touch screen to order. But I've been travelling a lot, and really the highlight of restaurant experience is often when talking to waitress. And don't get me wrong, it is most of the time broken telephone, but isn't this more fun? I always laugh with them about the situation and I don't know about you, but it fills me with good feeling. 

And yes, I understand, some of you have horrible anxieties and so on. I had them too. I was, and still largely remain, an introvert, I like to stay indoors and not talking to anyone. But since I was a child I understood that I don't want to live my life like that. I want to experience the human connection even thought it is hard for me all the time. And because I expose myself every day to these unconformable feelings, I get better at talking to people. I understand them better and so on. 

Connecting with people around you is a skill that can be improved, it only takes courage to get out of the bubble, because that bubble will eventually suffocate you.

**page:** 53-57
Author talks about project LifeNaut. This project core idea is that you talk to your personalized AI chatbot, and it learns from you how to be you. It learns your mannerism, the way you talk, behave and so on. In the end you (or better say LifeNaut) are left with a digital copy of yourself. 

I don't know about you, but when I heard about premise of this company, all my alarm bells rung so loudly in my brain. This brings up so many issues that author does not explore. And I understand that probably there could be written a whole book about it but still, in my opinion, it would be fair to talk about these issues. And I'm not talking only about data privacy, which gosh, there are a lot of problems. Where this data is stored, is it used only to train "your" chatbot or right about any, in the purpose of sounding better and more authentic. I'm also talking about the philosophical purpose here. She talks about how we are going to be able to speak to our deceased parents, friends, partners but to what purpose? In order to get over the death of someone close to us, we need to grief, and the option of talking to the dead is not grieving, its pushing grief to the side. Whilst substituting the emotional closure to some AI bot. It's disgusting exploration of grieving people. 

The project is "free", but there is nothing free in this world, especially coming from corporation. You pay with your data and that's the worst exchange.

Also, why would you want to engage with such project for reasons beyond your curiosity. The author raises a possibility of this being a step to "living forever". Why anyone would like to live forever if not being a millionaire or billionaire and so on. And okay, this means, we are most probably going to live forever in some kind of meta-verse where the chatbots with plus some brain scanning and so on will recreate us. But do you really want to put this "hell" on yourself? Do you really trust anyone to build a virtual space without some kind of twisted bug or subscription fee? Because I don't. Phones are full of ads and junk, we now have to pay a subscription in order to use ink in the printer you bought. You really think this is not going to be another episode of Black Mirror? I highly doubt that. 

Yes, it is very good to think about these things. It's fascinating. I love science fiction and to see some of my favourite genre coming to life in my generation is incredible. But I can't live with pink glasses all the time. We need to think critically about any new technology that crosses our way. AI has risen quite recently and what a mess it created. 

New technologies are necessary to push humanity forward. I myself am a researcher. But every technology can and will be used for something bad, it's just a human nature. And yes, no technology is "evil" per se, the evil aspect is added through the actions of users, but don't we have a moral obligation to make this Earth better and maintain a healthy society. 

**page:** 58
On this page we are introduced to the project and research by Calico Labs. Their research is focus on aging and how to prevent it or at least prolong our lifespans. I have no issue with that, it sounds as being deeply rooted in science, which I can only applaud.

But what about our minds? We probably can treat aging some way, because some animals exhibit this quality such as medusae, some crabs and tortoises. But I don't really hear anything about projects or at least something that would keep us sane throughout this new long life. Evolution gave us a biological purpose, to reproduce, take care of an offspring and die. Our minds keep us somewhat sane during this time. 

But let's imagine ourselves as a code or a program running day and night accumulating a lot of experiences, some good some bad. New day gives us new opportunities, whilst at night our brains work hard to categorise everything in the brain and come to conclusion that make us who we are. Body slowly dies. Brain doesn't have the brain activity it once had, so such back up and organisation is much more difficult with time. And so we accumulate errors. We don't remember as well as we once did, and so our personalities begin to shatter. And I'm not even talking about psychological issues that we are born with or/and we acquired them throughout the life. The code accumulates errors, bugs and one day it will stop working. We can replace everything in the body, be the Theseus ship if you will. But the mind can't be replaced, without it there is no us. 

I'm not critiquing the project, I'm just pointing out the gap in it.

### Second part: Death
In the second part of the book author touches upon death. How death is reshaped in our modern age with AI and for example VR technology.

For me, death is important part of a life. We all need to remember that we are not immortal and that eventually we and everyone and everything around us will die. *MEMENTO MORI* as one would say from the back seats. Death is part of every day life and so people try desperately to find a way to prolong our lives and fight death.

For the main part we did that already. We live longer than our ancestors and my kids and kids of my children will live even longer. But death will still linger. And you can't convince me that it is a bad thing. We humans are not meant to live long. Even now as we live past 80 we see much frequent emergence of illnesses like cancer, dementia, Alzheimer's and so on. Our bodies stop regenerate as efficiently as it once did. Our immune systems weaken and thus we are more prone to various quite horrible illnesses. Of course, longer we research these illnesses the more we understand them and so better we can fight them. But this research is much slower than expenditure of our lives.

So, in my opinion, we as a humanity should be spending more time in accepting the inevitability of death and teach kids what is death rather than trying to hide from it. We can't outrun death, but death gives opportunity we just need to see past our lives. I would say it is similar to how politicians operate versus how they really should work. When electing a politician they always make promises only to a foreseeable future but nothing long-term, because they probably won't stay for that long in the office anyway so why bother. But a lot of changes society and community they serve needs requires much more time. The problem is that nobody wants to do the hard and unpopular work of creating a healthy and strong foundation from which a more efficient community would rise. 

I would say the death of the individual is the same. We are so afraid of death because we think that once we die it is over for us and our legacy. But it is so not true. Just because of you living you've touched so many lives in bad and in good way, and it is truly like a ripple effect. Your actions will always stay here, because through them, you've changed somebodies life, and they change someone else's life and so on. And not just that, I think also a scientific community as a whole is another beautiful example of what I mean. Scientific discoveries, however small, will always stay here. Your contribution will never be forgotten, your name, maybe, but not your work. 

I'm not afraid of death. Maybe because I have stood right on the edge of life and returned, but mostly because I see my reason to live to help humanity, to continue the work of my ancestors, which is staying alive and making lives as beautiful as possible.

"By simulating scenarios of loss, resurrection, and consequence, games create a safe space for individuals to confront the unknown and experience the choices in the face of death. These virtual experiences mirror the purpose of tools such as living wills - offering a means to grapple with uncertainty, navigate agency, and find meaning within the constraints of inevitable mortality."

This is very beautifully said. I do agree with this premise fully. Games offer such a huge variety in usage so why not use it to explore death, and it's meaning. I know I will sound like a broken record, but I love how Life Is Strange and The Talos Principle explore the theme of death. 

Life Is Strange is literally a game where main heroine goes through a grief of loosing her best friend. Quite a lot of people speculate that the whole game is just her brain trying to get over the death of Chloe so it came up with the story of time travel so she can explore the way she feels about it and try to move on. 

On the other hand, The Talos Principle explores an idea of death more philosophically. We play and explore the world through the eyes of a robot, so death is not really an issue for us, because we are going to live for a long, long time. But still, in order to understand meaning of life we need to understand meaning and significance of death. That is the main premise of the game, this is what The Talos Principle about. 

The game centers around the giant Talos, who had one vein through which his blood was flowing. The message is simple: all can bleed and all can die. But the game reframes death as an opportunity for humanity. This is actually also explored in Project Hail Mary. We all participate in a long project called humanity. This project expands beyond one human life. And because of how short our lives are in a grand scheme of things we have learnt how to share the knowledge we accumulate throughout our lives. 

But in order to be a "good and valuable" participant of this project, you need to think beyond your life and this also means accepting your death. To create a better society we need to at least sometimes look beyond our own immediate happiness and think of our common future. Just imagine a society where majority of people would engage even with the smallest actions with this future. Don't you think we all would be better for it?

**page:** 142
"What does heaven look like to you?"

This is a question that the author gets when teaching her course on "Death and Dying" and my goodness I love this question. I remember when battling about my religious beliefs when I was younger (as also written [here](https://alchemistsnotes.com/notes/synthesizer/why-atheist/)) I was thinking a lot about heaven and hell. And I remember one video I saw that changed my view entirely. It was a video by a stand-up comic, and he was telling a bit about his grandfather. His grandfather had experienced a stroke but was brought back to life after few minutes of resuscitation. And he asked him what did he see when being dead. And his grandfather told him how he saw Lenin, how he was in a great beautiful amphitheater (similar to one in his hometown) and how he saw Mother Mary that came to him to take him to the other side. And the comic said how bizarre that was for him at first but then he understood something. That is that when dying our brains will do anything to soothe our pain and make transition to death as manageable as possible. And in those last moment of living brain will show us the best thing that we had ever seen. For his grandfather it was Lenin in that amphitheater, for somebody it is going to be Buddhist temple and so on. But the point is, that there is nothing out there, we just see the last images our brains create with the help of last doses of hormones. In these moments we are happy, because this is how our personal heaven looks like. Just a mixture of greatest places and people we have seen throughout our lives in one place.

*She writes this notion:*
**page:** 143
"Heavens - and afterlife conceptions in general - are constructed and often idealized realities meant to complement our everyday lives, offering meaning, community, understanding, and augmentation to our everyday world."

### Appendix
**At the end of a book** there are declarations that are there to view and sign. These declarations are about the body and your digital assets. 

I think it is very important to know whether you want to receive life support and what you want to happen with your data. And I think it is very important to talk about that with your partner, parents and your immediate circle, so everybody is on the same page when it comes to your desires about how you want to continue to live or how you want to die.
